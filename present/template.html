When it rains,Where should taxis go?

A data story for ComfortDelGro · DAO2702
Rain light to heavy vacant taxi · illustrative animation
Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors
Our question is simple: when it rains in Singapore, where do vacant taxis go, and where should they be? We are a student team, and this project is not affiliated with ComfortDelGro. Everything we show comes from public data: five years of taxi positions from the government feed, plus rainfall from NEA gauges and forecasts. Two words matter throughout. First, we see vacant taxis, which is supply; we cannot see riders, bookings or waiting times. Second, because this is observational data, the patterns we show are associations, not proof that rain causes them. The moving rain and taxi dots on this slide are an illustration, not data. Let us start with the market.

Source: LTA (2026), vehicle population 2015 and 2025 (the line joins these two years only); The Straits Times (2026).
Singapore's taxi fleet has shrunk sharply. LTA counts show 28,259 taxis in 2015 and 12,161 in 2025, while private-hire cars grew from 29,369 to 95,857. The line simply joins those two years; we are not showing the path in between. The Straits Times reported that ComfortDelGro's local fleet fell about 5 per cent in the first half of 2026. With fewer taxis, each one matters more on a rainy day. So our business question is: where, when and how many available taxis should be positioned during rain? Public sources describe matching that reacts to bookings; we found nothing public about positioning taxis ahead of rain.

We worked in five steps. First, we cleaned the data: we audited feed outages, stuck rain gauges and unusual days. Second, we compared rainy and dry half-hours with Poisson regression, holding the square, time of day and season fixed. Third, we checked the results with bootstraps and false-discovery tests, so a few lucky squares cannot drive the story. Fourth, we forecast short-term shortages with LightGBM and compared it with a simple rule that assumes nothing changes. Fifth, we turned the results into an optimisation plan. Three limits apply to everything: one combined public feed with no taxi IDs, vacant taxis only, and associations rather than causes.

Here is one real storm, on 20 July 2023. The left map shows rain in the previous half-hour; grey means dry. The right map compares vacant taxis with each square's usual level. At the 09:30 peak there were 1,473 vacant taxis across our 374 squares against about 2,310 expected: 837 fewer, or 36.2 per cent. On average, light rain goes with 7.7 per cent fewer vacant taxis, moderate with 10.8 and heavy with 12.9, and the CBD with 24.5 per cent. Against the city at the same moment, the extra local drop is only 5.7 per cent, so most of the drop is citywide. These are associations.

When does the drop happen? This chart lines up rain onsets in each square and compares the square with itself 30 minutes before rain begins. The gap is deepest about 30 minutes after rain starts, 5.6 per cent, and is no longer detectable after about two and a half hours. A separate model shows 7 per cent fewer vacant taxis when heavier rain is 2 to 5 kilometres away and the square is still dry. Even 1 millimetre per hour goes with 9.7 per cent fewer, and after two hours of heavy rain the gap reaches 18 per cent. After rain stops, supply stays about 5 per cent low for half an hour.

Where is the loss largest? The bars show how many fewer vacant taxis we see in a heavy-rain half-hour, and the map shows the percentage change in every square, with the same twelve places numbered. Why the CBD? We tested whether features of a square go with a bigger heavy-rain drop. Only the URA Central Area stands out: 24.5 per cent fewer vacant taxis against 12.0 per cent elsewhere, and 18.9 against 5.0 per cent in the share model. Near MRT exits, taxi stands or malls the drop stays between 11.6 and 12.5 per cent. Square-level models agree, with about 11 per cent more loss in the Central Area and smaller losses on business land. Taxi stands show about 2.5 per cent per standard deviation in one design only. A random forest explains little out of area, R squared 0.12 to 0.15.

Can we plan for it? This heatmap shows, on weekdays, how many fewer vacant taxis we see per half-hour when NEA's issued forecast mentions showers. Changi from midnight to 7 am averages 33.1 fewer, or 23 per cent; Downtown Core 20.2 fewer, 43 per cent; Bukit Merah from 5 pm to midnight 21.4 and 24.8 fewer, about 28 per cent. Negative cells mean more taxis than usual. For short-term alerts, weather adds about 0.001 PR-AUC; LightGBM is right 53 per cent of the time against 46 for 'nothing changes', and only 6 to 13 per cent of shortages are flagged in advance. The morning 24-hour forecast improves later predictions by 6 to 10 per cent.

Here is the playbook. By 9 am, read NEA's 24-hour forecast and schedule incentives; it plans 6 to 10 per cent better. Thirty to sixty minutes before rain, when it is 2 to 5 kilometres away, move idle drivers toward the CBD. During rain, focus on Changi and Downtown Core early, Geylang and Kallang at rush hour, and Bukit Merah in the evening. In island-wide rain, get more drivers out rather than shuffling taxis. Step up support in long spells and keep incentives for one to two hours after. Our plan picks short squares 44 per cent of the time, against 28. Don't spend on rain-triggered real-time alerts: weather adds only 0.001 PR-AUC.

Limitations

What our data can't tell us

Supply, not demandNo waiting times or fares
Associations, not causesRain coincides with fewer vacant taxis; we cannot say why
No taxi IDsCDG can't be separated from other operators
The simulator can be more robustUse it to illustrate, not to predict

Checked with 100 m radar rainfall, permutation tests, and fresh holdout data from 2026 and 2019.

Radar comparison, permutation inference, October 2026 and 2019 holdout storms. Details in appendix A3, A6 and A8.
Four limits shape how to use these results. First, we observe supply, not demand: we cannot see riders, waiting times or fares. Second, every rain result is an association; rain coincides with fewer vacant taxis, but our data cannot say why. Third, the public feed has no taxi IDs, so ComfortDelGro's taxis cannot be separated from other operators. Fourth, the simulator can be more robust, so we use it to illustrate, not to predict. We did test the main findings hard: with 100 metre radar rainfall, permutation tests, and fresh holdout data from 2026 and from 2019, and the core pattern held.

Appendix A2

Methods and definitions

Poisson fixed effects. Vacant taxis per square and half-hour, compared with the same square, time and season when dry.

Share model. The same for a square's share of all vacant taxis, separating local from citywide drops.

Event study. Half-hours around each rain onset, relative to 30 minutes before.

Bootstrap. Resamples whole days or storms for confidence intervals.

Permutation and false-discovery control. Shuffled rain days test chance; named places pass q < 0.10 with ≥ 4 expected taxis.

LightGBM vs persistence. Tree model vs "nothing changes", 30 or 60 minutes ahead.

PR-AUC. Ranking skill for real shortages; weather gain = with minus without weather.

Optimisation. Picks squares for repositioned taxis by forecast risk, within staging and travel limits.

Agent-based simulation. Simulated taxis on real roads; fails the realism check.

Time split. Fit Apr 2022–Mar 2025 · tune Apr–Sep 2025 · test Oct 2025–Sep 2026.

Appendix A9

Glossary

Vacant taxi. Shown as available in LTA's feed; supply, not riders.

Expected level. A square's usual level at that time when dry.

Heavy rain. More than 10 mm/h in the previous half-hour.

Association. Things move together; not proof of cause.

Square. One of 374 one-km grid squares.

Citywide share. A square's fraction of all vacant taxis.

95% CI. Range of values consistent with the data.

q-value. p-value adjusted for testing many places.

PR-AUC. Ranking skill for rare shortages.

Persistence. The "nothing changes" forecast.

Top-19 alerts. The 19 squares flagged as most at risk.

Issue-time label. Judged only with information available at forecast time.

Holdout. Data kept aside and scored once.
