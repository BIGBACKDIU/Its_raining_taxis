When it rains,
where should taxis go?

A data story for ComfortDelGro · DAO2702

Student project · not affiliated with ComfortDelGro

Our question is simple: when it rains in Singapore, where do vacant taxis go, and where should they be? We are a student team, and this project is not affiliated with ComfortDelGro. Everything we show comes from public data: five years of taxi positions from the government feed, plus rainfall from NEA gauges and forecasts. Two words matter throughout. First, we see vacant taxis, which is supply; we cannot see riders, bookings or waiting times. Second, because this is observational data, the patterns we show are associations, not proof that rain causes them. The moving rain on this map is decoration for the title only. Let us start with the market.
Context

Source: LTA (2026), vehicle population 2015 and 2025 (the line joins these two years only); The Straits Times (2026).
Singapore's taxi fleet has shrunk sharply. LTA counts show 28,259 taxis in 2015 and 12,161 in 2025, while private-hire cars grew from 29,369 to 95,857. The line simply joins those two years; we are not showing the path in between. The Straits Times reported that ComfortDelGro's local fleet fell about 5 per cent in the first half of 2026. With fewer taxis, each one matters more on a rainy day. So our business question is: where, when and how many available taxis should be positioned during rain? Public sources describe matching that reacts to bookings; we found nothing public about positioning taxis ahead of rain.

We worked in five steps. First, we cleaned the data: we audited feed outages, stuck rain gauges and unusual days. Second, we compared rainy and dry half-hours with Poisson regression, holding the square, time of day and season fixed. Third, we checked the results with bootstraps and false-discovery tests, so a few lucky squares cannot drive the story. Fourth, we forecast short-term shortages with LightGBM and compared it with a simple rule that assumes nothing changes. Fifth, we turned the results into an optimisation plan. Three limits apply to everything: one combined public feed with no taxi IDs, vacant taxis only, and associations rather than causes.

Here is one real storm, on 20 July 2023. The left map shows rain in the previous half-hour; grey means dry. The right map compares vacant taxis with the usual level for that square and time. At the 09:30 peak there were 1,473 vacant taxis across our 374 squares, against about 2,310 expected: 837 fewer, or 36.2 per cent. That is one storm. On average across 2022 to 2025, heavy rain comes with 12.9 per cent fewer vacant taxis, and 24.5 per cent fewer in the Central Area. These are associations: rain coincides with fewer vacant taxis, for reasons our data cannot separate.

When does the drop happen? This chart lines up rain onsets in each square and compares the square with itself 30 minutes before rain begins. Vacant taxis are already about 5 per cent lower in the first half-hour, stay low through the first hour, and the gap shrinks until about two and a half hours, when it is no longer distinguishable from noise. A separate model adds something useful for planning: when heavier rain is 2 to 5 kilometres away and the square itself is still dry, there are already about 7 per cent fewer vacant taxis. So the window to act is before the rain reaches a place.

Where is the loss largest? Each bar shows how many fewer vacant taxis we see in a heavy-rain half-hour, with the expected number and the percentage. Changi Terminal 3 loses the most taxis, 7.1 of about 83 expected, but that is only 8.5 per cent because the airport has so many. Chart 4 · Diagnostic

Only the CBD stands out

Tested three ways (regression, spatial model, random forest): only the Central Area holds up in all three.
MRT exits, taxi stands and malls make no clear difference.
Heavy-rain change in vacant taxis vs dry, by square characteristic, with 95% CIs; dotted line = reference (MRT exit ≥ 1 km). Associations, not causes.
Why the CBD? We tested whether common features of a square go with a bigger heavy-rain drop. Squares more than a kilometre from an MRT exit show about 12.0 per cent fewer vacant taxis in heavy rain. Squares near MRT exits, near taxi stands or with a mall are almost identical, between 11.6 and 12.5 per cent. Only the URA Central Area stands out, at 24.5 per cent, with a confidence interval well clear of the others. We checked this three ways, with the regression, a spatial model and a random forest, and only the Central Area held up in all three. These are associations, not causes.

Can we plan for it? This heatmap shows, for weekdays, how many fewer vacant taxis we see per half-hour when NEA's issued forecast mentions showers, by planning area and time band. Changi between midnight and 7 am averages 33.1 fewer, or 23 per cent; Downtown Core 20.2 fewer, or 43 per cent; Bukit Merah in the evening 21.4 and 24.8 fewer, about 28 per cent. Negative cells mean more taxis than usual. For short-term alerts, weather adds almost nothing, about 0.001 PR-AUC, and only 6 to 13 per cent of shortages are flagged in advance. But NEA's morning 24-hour forecast improves predictions of later shortages by 6 to 10 per cent.

Recommendation

A rain-day playbook

By 09:00

Read the 24-hour forecast; schedule incentives and staging.

30–60 min before

Rain forecast or 2–5 km away: move idle drivers toward the CBD.

During rain

Changi and Downtown Core early; Geylang and Kallang at rush hour; Bukit Merah in the evening.

Island-wide rain

Get more drivers out; don't move taxis between areas that are all short.

Over 2 hours

Step up support.

After rain

Keep incentives for 1–2 hours.

{{policy}}Our repositioning plan picks squares that really run short {{policy}} of the time, vs {{random}} at random.
Plan precision: risk-weighted repositioning plan vs all candidate squares (expected ≥ 4 taxis), test year, issue-time labels. Timing from the event study; places from the planning heatmap.
Here is how the findings become a playbook. By 9 am, read NEA's 24-hour forecast and schedule incentives and staging. Thirty to sixty minutes before rain, when it is forecast or 2 to 5 kilometres away, move idle drivers toward the CBD. During rain, focus on Changi and Downtown Core early in the day, Geylang and Kallang at rush hour, and Bukit Merah in the evening. When rain is island-wide, get more drivers out rather than moving taxis between areas that are all short. For long spells, step up support, and keep incentives for one to two hours afterwards. Our plan picks squares that really run short 44 per cent of the time, against 28 per cent for all candidates.

Robustness: radar comparison, permutation inference, October 2026 and 2019 holdout storms.
Four limits shape how to use these results. First, we observe supply, not demand: we cannot see riders, waiting times or fares. Second, every rain result is an association; rain coincides with fewer vacant taxis, but our data cannot say why. Third, the public feed has no taxi IDs, so ComfortDelGro's taxis cannot be separated from other operators. Fourth, the simulator fails its realism check on real storms, so it illustrates and does not predict. We did test the main findings hard: with 100 metre radar rainfall, permutation tests, and fresh holdout data from 2026 and from 2019, and the core pattern held.

Where could this go next? First, prove it works: pilot the playbook on some rain days or zones and not others, then compare taxis, pickups and waiting times, which a pilot with company data could measure. Second, close the data gap: bookings, unfulfilled requests and driver status would show why taxis disappear; without taxi IDs, our minute-by-minute tracking was only 10 to 23 per cent accurate. Third, a better short-term signal: NEA's radar API, live since July 2026, could support regional storm alerts; in the 2019 storms, weather gave a real but small gain of 0.009 PR-AUC. Fourth, look beyond rain: MRT breakdowns, big events, and dry-weather taxi deserts such as Punggol.

To close, three things to remember. One: heavy rain reliably comes with about 13 per cent fewer vacant taxis on the street, and about 25 per cent fewer in the CBD; that is an association we found across five years, not a proven cause. Two: act early. The useful signal is the morning forecast, which helps plan the day, not short-term weather alerts, which add very little. Three: the next step is ComfortDelGro's own data and a pilot, which together could show whether the playbook really helps. Thank you; we are happy to take questions.
