THE QUESTION BEHIND THE MAP

Less supply in rain.
But can we act early?

ComfortDelGro needs to know where vacant taxis are likely to thin out. We matched Singapore’s public taxi snapshots to recent rainfall and forecasts, then separated three questions.

01 How much changes?02 Where and when?03 Does weather warn us?
READ THIS FIRST

The feed covers all operators and has no taxi or company IDs. It measures available supply. It cannot measure passenger queues, identify hires or show why a driver disappears.

01 / THE ASSOCIATION

Heavier rain.
Fewer vacant taxis.

Compare each square with itself at a similar time in dry weather. Heavy rain is associated with about 13% fewer vacant taxis.

A MEASUREMENT CHECK

Would a finer rain map change the answer?

Gauges can miss local showers. We repeated the comparison using 100 m radar rainfall, on the same reduced sample.

−12.1% gauge
−12.0% radar

The heavy-rain result barely moves. Light rain changes more. The radar product is adjusted using gauges, so agreement is a consistency check.

02 / THE TIMING

The warning signs
can arrive before the rain.

Nearby rain and issued forecasts are associated with lower supply even while a square remains dry. That is an association—not proof that drivers saw the forecast.

03 / THE GEOGRAPHY

Two different answers
to “where is worst?”

Changi loses the most taxis in number. The Central Area has larger percentage losses. Counts and percentages answer different planning questions.

WHEN RAIN IS WIDESPREAD

Gaining share does not necessarily mean gaining taxis.

329of 374 squares clearly lose taxis

Only three clearly gain in absolute counts. Broad losses limit the headroom for repositioning; they do not prove that every possible move is ineffective.

04 / THE MAIN TEST

Finding a pattern
is easier than forecasting it.

Does weather add information beyond the taxi count we can already see? That is the practical early-warning test.

05 / WHERE WEATHER CAN HELP

Plan the day.
Test the response.

The morning forecast helps a flexible model predict later low-supply periods. It provides a reason to test planning interventions—not a measured benefit from incentives.

CANDIDATES FOR AN OPERATIONAL TRIAL

Use the geography.
Measure what changes.

  1. Prepare from the morning forecast.

    Use the expected timing and places to plan staffing or staging experiments.

  2. Prioritise supply gaps by time band.

    Changi early, Bukit Merah later, and CBD/fringe during weekday rush hours. Keep counts beside percentages.

  3. Evaluate the intervention itself.

    The saved targeting check is 44.2% versus 27.7% among all candidate cells, with different group sizes. It does not establish that moving drivers improves outcomes.

THE PRACTICAL CONCLUSION

We know where supply thins.
We still need to know why.

Public data supports the rain association and the geography. Booking records and driver-status data are needed to explain the mechanism and evaluate a real dispatch response.

How to read the methods and limits

Coverage is part of the data

The feed changes coverage. The audit identifies regimes and exclusions. Main models account for time and coverage; share models compare cells with the city at the same moment. This supports interpretation but does not identify causality.

Time comes first

Rain uses (t−30,t], with earlier windows as lags. Forecasts are used as issued. Samples and label versions are shown beside the charts. Corrective and additional test uses are logged.

Uncertainty stays visible

Intervals are saved 95% CIs. Named rain rankings require expected supply ≥4 and q<0.10. Recovery is a statistical criterion. Results apply to the tested models, labels and horizons.

Frozen evidence

This page only renders existing result tables and saved figures. It performs no estimation, tuning, model scoring or new holdout-data access. The appendix retains original diagnostics, weak designs and null findings.
